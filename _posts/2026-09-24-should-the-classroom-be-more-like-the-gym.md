When I’m in New York, I take spin classes on the Upper East Side. The students—it’s a class, after all—pack into a dark, humid room. The music is loud. The air reeks of effort. When the black light hits, our towels fluoresce. “I can do that!” we shout, answering the instructor’s call. Everyone is working.

在纽约逗留期间，我常去上东区的一家动感单车房上课。学员们——毕竟这算是一门课——挤在一个幽暗而潮湿的房间里。音乐震耳欲聋，空气中弥漫着全力以赴的汗水气息。紫光灯扫过时，我们的毛巾泛起荧光。“我可以做到！”我们齐声高呼，回应着教练的号召。每个人都在拼尽全力。

It is not especially pleasant. It is not supposed to be. We have chosen, quite deliberately, to subject ourselves to forty-five minutes of physical discomfort for benefits accrued down the road: better health, greater strength, a future version of ourselves that will justify the soreness tomorrow morning. Moreover, we are doing this by engaging in an activity that, in the real world, we might very well avoid because there are perfectly good machines to do it for us. A car or an e-bike can more easily get you from point A to point B. Yet no one in the class doubts that what we are doing is necessary. No one would dream of outsourcing it. It wouldn’t make sense.

这并不怎么令人愉悦，而且本来也不该令人愉悦。我们心甘情愿、深思熟虑地让自己承受这四十五分钟的肉体折磨，是为了换取未来的长远收益：更健康的体魄、更强健的力量，以及一个能让明晨的肌肉酸痛显得完全物有所值的未来的自己。更重要的是，我们所从事的这项运动，在现实生活中本可以完全避开——因为有现成的精良机器可以代劳。汽车或电动自行车能轻而易举地将你从 A 点运送到 B 点。然而，课堂上没有一个人会怀疑我们此刻所做的事是否有其必要，也没有人会异想天开地把这项锻炼“外包”出去。因为那样做毫无意义。

I’ve been thinking about that room a lot lately, because I spend most of my professional life in a different kind of room entirely—the university classroom—and, as with the spin class, we now have machines that can do much of what we ask students to do in the name of training. Unlike with spinning, the effect has been to call the whole enterprise into question. Rather than “I can do that!,” the refrain I hear most often from students and colleagues is: “Why are we doing that?” I’m starting to think that the gym, of all places, might have some answers.

最近我常常想起那个房间，因为我职业生涯的绝大部分时间都在另一种截然不同的房间度过——大学课堂。就像动感单车课一样，我们如今也拥有了强大的机器，能够代劳我们在训练名义下要求学生所做的大部分事情。但与动感单车不同的是，技术带来的后果是让整个教育事业陷入了存在性质疑。从学生和同事口中，我听到最多的不再是“我可以做到！”，而是困惑的质问：“我们到底为什么要做这个？”我开始觉得，在所有可能的地方中，健身房或许恰恰藏着某些答案。

I teach mathematics and computer science at Dartmouth, and, on the first day of a large introductory course, I always ask the students to tell me, by a show of hands, how many of them are skilled at a sport or an instrument. It’s usually all of them. I ask whether they had to practice to become good, a question that is met with lots of nodding and a hum of general assent. The same is true for math, I tell them, and by extension for critical thinking, for writing, for argument. They nod. They understand the analogy immediately and completely.

我在达特茅斯学院（Dartmouth）教授数学与计算机科学。在一门大型入门课程的第一堂课上，我总是会让学生们举手示意，统计有多少人擅长某项体育运动或某种乐器。通常全班都会举手。我接着问他们，是否必须经过刻苦练习才能达到精通？这个问题总能收获一片点头和赞同的低语。我告诉他们：数学也是如此；引申开来，批判性思维、写作和逻辑辩论同样如此。他们纷纷点头，瞬间心领神会了这个比喻。

They agree, but many of them go home and feed their work to their favorite [large language model](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/02/16/what-is-claude-anthropic-doesnt-know-either). I don’t entirely blame them. We built the system that made it logical. For years, the architecture of higher education quietly encouraged students to believe that the point of a class, and indeed of the entire college experience, was the credential—that the teacher-student relationship was a transactional one that ended with a grade. But if the grade is the goal, and a machine can get you the grade, the machine is not a shortcut; it’s just efficiency.

他们嘴上赞同，但很多人回到家后，转头就把作业一股脑塞进了自己最常用的[大语言模型](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/02/16/what-is-claude-anthropic-doesnt-know-either)。我并不完全责怪他们，因为正是我们亲手打造的这套评价体制让这一切显得顺理成章。多年来，高等教育的制度架构一直在潜移默化地诱导学生相信：一门课程乃至整个大学生涯的核心意义就在于文凭与凭证——师生关系不过是一场以成绩单为终点的交易。然而，如果成绩本身就是终极目标，而机器能够替你拿到这个成绩，那么机器就不仅是一条捷径，而是成了纯粹的高效手段。

Early this year, an A.I. tool called Einstein was announced. It could allegedly integrate with Canvas—a learning-management system used at thousands of universities—to ingest homework assignments, complete them, and submit them on a student’s behalf. It would write blog posts and even engage in professor-student exchanges, all while you slept: “Einstein does the busywork so you won’t have to.” The only feature missing was a premium tier that would argue with the professor about your grade.

今年初，一款名为 Einstein 的 AI 工具高调面世。据称，它能与数千所大学通用的学习管理系统 Canvas 深度集成，自动抓取课后作业、代为完成并替学生提交。它甚至能在你安睡之际撰写随笔博客，并替你与教授进行日常答疑互动，其宣传语直截了当：“让爱因斯坦去应付琐碎杂务，你只管高枕无忧。”它唯一缺少的，大概就是一个能替你跟教授就成绩讨价还价的高级付费会员功能了。

The product was almost certainly hype. (It vanished from the web almost as quickly as it appeared.) But it was clarifying hype. Any outrage that the announcement generated—“next level A.I. shit” was the subject line of an e-mail that zinged around my network—tees up a natural question: Outrage at what, exactly? That a machine would do the homework? Or that the homework was apparently so mechanical that a machine could do it at all? One of my colleagues wrote, “What is learning outside of busywork?”

这款产品几乎可以断定是一场炒作（它在互联网上销声匿迹的速度与其登场时一样迅疾）。但这场炒作却带来了一种清醒剂般的启示。该发布引发的一切义愤——一封在我朋友圈中疯传的邮件标题赫然写着“AI 离谱到了全新高度”——自然牵引出一个根本性的叩问：我们到底在愤怒什么？是愤怒机器竟然代做了作业？还是愤怒这些作业本身竟然机械到了机器随随便便就能代劳的地步？我的一位同事写道：“除去这些机械琐务，学习还剩下什么？”

I know what he means, even if he meant it ironically. As teachers, the tasks we assign are never really the point. We keep the students “busy” with tasks: do the homework, write the essay, work toward the grade, and, whether or not you are aware of it, you’ll be building skills. In the past, we were more or less comfortable with this carrot-and-stick arrangement, because we were confident that students would be honing their minds. But large language models and the long shadow of the job market—are we grading for the student or for the gatekeepers beyond graduation?—have disrupted a centuries-old educational paradigm. The stick is broken and the carrot has lost its taste. If the *process* is the goal, we should be focussing on how to create classrooms that make the process feel worth doing for its own sake.

哪怕他带着讽刺的口吻，我也深知他的弦外之音。作为教师，我们布置的那些课业任务从来都不是目的本身。我们用各种任务让学生“忙碌”起来：完成作业、撰写论文、为好成绩努力；而无论你是否意识到，你都在这个过程中锤炼着自己的能力。在过去，我们大体上安于这种胡萝卜加大棒的机制，因为我们确信学生在此过程中终将磨砺心智。然而，大语言模型的横空出世以及严峻就业市场投下的漫长阴影——我们打分到底是为了学生本身，还是为了他们毕业后面对的那些行业守门人？——彻底击碎了维系百年的教育范式。大棒已经折断，胡萝卜也味同嚼蜡。如果*过程*本身才是真正的目标，那么我们当下应当聚焦的核心，是如何打造出一种让学生感到“过程本身就值得为之全心投入”的课堂。

Which brings me back to the gym. No one does a bench press because they expect to spend their life pushing bars off of their chests. The bench press is a proxy—a way of building underlying strength that will serve you in ways you can’t fully anticipate. A problem set, a close-reading exercise, a research paper: these were always proxies, too. Einstein—the physicist, not the software product—gave a remarkable address in 1936, on the three-hundredth anniversary of higher education in America, in which he reminded his audience of a hard-to-source aphorism: “Education is what remains if one has forgotten everything he learned in school.” The specifics of your coursework aren’t what’s valuable—what’s valuable are the habits of mind that education can cultivate, what Einstein described as the “general ability for independent thinking and judgment.” We learn how to think, even if we don’t exactly recall what we were thinking about decades ago.

这便又把我引回了健身房。没有人做卧推是因为指望自己毕生都要把杠铃从胸口推开。卧推只是一种“代偿载体”（proxy）——一种培养底层力量的方式，而这种力量未来将在你无法完全预见的情境中发挥效用。一套数学题集、一次文本细读练习、一篇研究论文：它们从一开始也同样是这种代偿载体。爱因斯坦——真正的物理学家，而非那个软件产品——在 1936 年美国高等教育三百周年庆典上发表过一次著名的演讲，其中他援引了一句出处难以确证的名言：“所谓教育，就是一个人把在学校学到的所有具体知识忘光之后所剩下的东西。”课程中细枝末节的知识点从来不是最具价值的部分；真正宝贵的，是教育所能培育的心智习惯，也就是爱因斯坦所说的“独立思考与独立判断的通识能力”。我们学会的是如何思考，即便几十年后我们早已记不清当年到底在具体琢磨些什么。

We take it upon ourselves to do the bench press, to ride the exercise bike. But we try to avoid the homework. Why? Some of it, I think, comes down to feedback. The body offers something that the mind—at least in a traditional classroom—rarely does: clear, immediate loops linking effort and result. In the gym, you feel the burn, see the muscle respond, track the weight going up week by week. The resistance knob on a spin bike gives you a continuous, private metric of effort. The feedback is embodied. But, when you are wrestling with a complicated text or idea, it can be days or even weeks before the clouds begin to clear. In the gym, we can steadily feel ourselves getting stronger. In the classroom, progress can be difficult to see.

我们心甘情愿地去卧推、去踩动感单车，却千方百计地逃避作业。这是为什么？我认为，在很大程度上要归结于“反馈机制”。身体能够提供某种心智——至少在传统课堂中——极少能给予的东西：即刻而清晰地将付出与收获连接起来的即时闭环。在健身房里，你能感受到肌肉的灼烧感，看到肌群充血膨胀，记录每周不断攀升的重量。动感单车上的阻力调节旋钮，为你提供了一个连续且私密的努力度量衡。这种反馈是具身化的（embodied）。然而，当你艰难啃读一篇晦涩的文本或深奥的理论时，往往需要几天甚至数周的时间才能拨云见日。在健身房，我们能切实感受到自己正稳步变强；而在课堂上，进步往往难以捉摸、难以目睹。

As a kid in the early nineteen-seventies, I learned to “read for comprehension” through something called the S.R.A. Reading Laboratory—a box of sequenced, color-coded cards, which, as I moved from one color to the next, gave me a small, visible marker of progress. It was, in retrospect, a gym for reading: you worked at your own pace, you felt yourself improving, and the improvement was legible. One colleague of mine, unwilling to wait for that legibility to arrive on its own, has redesigned some of his courses around a core principle: he lets students retake exams until the concepts finally click (or as often as his own schedule permits). This is not because he’s indulgent but because he treats mastery the way a coach treats a skill, as something built through repetition, correction, and return. The grade, in his classroom, is a by-product of learning rather than a substitute for it.

上世纪七十年代初我还是个孩子时，曾通过一种名为 SRA 阅读实验室（S.R.A. Reading Laboratory）的教具来练习“阅读理解”——那是一盒按颜色编排分级的卡片；每当我从一种颜色晋级到下一种颜色，就获得了一个微小而清晰的进步标记。回想起来，那就像是一个阅读健身房：你按自己的节奏训练，能真切感受到自己的进步，而且这种进步是直观可见的。我的一位同事不愿坐等这种可见性自发降临，便围绕一个核心理念重构了自己的部分课程：他允许学生反复补考，直到真正融会贯通相关概念为止（或者在他自己的日程允许范围内尽可能多次）。这绝非出于姑息纵容，而是因为他像教练对待运动技能那样对待知识掌握——将其视作通过重复、纠错与循环往复所逐步构筑的东西。在他的课堂里，成绩只是学习的副产品，而非学习的替代品。

The great challenge of teaching is not to transmit information—machines do that cheaply now—but to engineer those moments of genuine understanding reliably enough that students come to crave them. The intellectual equivalent of the body’s feedback is real and, when it arrives, more powerful than any endorphin. It is the “Aha!” of a hard problem suddenly solved, a proof that clicks into place, the moment when an argument you’ve been wrestling with finally holds its own weight. I have seen it on students’ faces, and there’s nothing quite like it. “The most important motive for work in the school and in life is pleasure in work, pleasure in its results, and the knowledge of the value of the result to the community,” Einstein said. “In the awakening and strengthening of the psychological forces in the young man, I see the most important task.” That’s what the best coaches have always known. And it’s what the best teachers have always striven for.

教学的巨大挑战从来不是传递信息——如今机器能以极低的成本完成这一步——而是如何足够稳定可靠地促成那些“真正豁然开朗的顿悟时刻”，让学生发自内心地渴望它们。肉体反馈在智力层面的对应物是真实存在的，而且一旦降临，它比任何内啡肽都更为强劲。那是久攻不下的难题迎刃而解时的“豁然开朗”（Aha!），是一个数学证明各环节严丝合缝扣合到位的快意，是你苦苦思索的论证终于能够自圆其说的刹那。我曾在学生的脸庞上无数次目睹这种神采，世上没有任何体验能与之媲美。“在学校与生活之中，工作最重要的驱动力是沉浸于工作本身的乐趣、享受其成果的欣喜，以及深知这些成果对群体具有价值的笃定，”爱因斯坦曾言，“在唤醒与强化年轻一代内心的精神力量中，我看到了最为至关重要的使命。”最优秀的教练深谙此道，最优秀的教师也向来以此为毕生追求。

The ancient Greeks did not separate the training of body and mind as cleanly as we do. Their *gymnasion* was simultaneously a place of athletic exercise and of philosophical exchange; Plato taught in one. Centuries later, the German *gymnasium* became synonymous not with athletics but with intellectual rigor. Somewhere along the way we shortened the word and split the practice. Society treats physical and intellectual training as though they obey different laws—as though the mind, unlike the body, might get strong without being made to struggle.

古希腊人并未像我们今天这样，把身与心的磨砺割裂得如此泾渭分明。他们的 *gymnasion*（角力学校/体艺馆）既是体育竞技锻炼的场所，也是哲学思辨交锋的殿堂；柏拉图便曾在此授徒讲学。数百年后，德语中的 *Gymnasium*（文理中学）不再与体育运动画等号，而是成了严谨智识训练的代名词。但在漫长的演化道路上的某个时刻，我们缩短了这个词，也割裂了这项实践。当今社会看待肉体锻炼与智识磨砺，仿佛它们遵循着完全不同的法则——就好像心智与肉体不同，不必经历挣扎与痛苦就能凭空变得强韧似的。

It doesn’t. Aristotle observed that we become virtuous by performing virtuous acts—that character is cultivated through practice. A similar logic applies to thinking. Intellectual strength isn’t transmitted by lecture or implanted with a diploma. It is built the same way that the figure skater builds a routine or the running back learns to explode through a crack in the line—repetition after repetition, failure after failure, with no shortcut available and no machine that can stand in.

事实绝非如此。亚里士多德曾洞察到：我们是通过躬行善举才变得品德高尚——品格是在反复实践中培育出来的。思考同样遵循着完全相同的逻辑。智力维度的强健从不可能仅靠听讲座来传递，也不可能随着一纸文凭而被植入脑海。它的铸就过程，与花样滑冰选手打磨一套动作、橄榄球跑卫学会从防线夹缝中悍然突破别无二致——一次又一次的重复，一次又一次的跌倒，绝无任何捷径可走，也绝无任何机器能够代劳。

The Greeks even had a name for it. The *progymnasmata* were a sequence of rhetorical exercises, graduated in difficulty, designed to build a student’s capacity for argument, the way a training program builds a runner’s endurance: incrementally, with each exercise preparing the ground for the next. The goal was not a finished piece of writing but a strengthened mind. Kyle Booten, a poet and an English assistant professor at the University of Connecticut, has spent the past several years developing a digital return to this tradition—computational tools designed to make writing not easier but harder, to put resistance back into the act of composition, the way a coach adds weight to a bar. He has a program that demands the use of rare words. Another glorious experiment is his Lotus Chorus Workshop, in which prompts challenge the user to try out all kinds of strange sentence constructions, like a literary CrossFit box. Appropriately, Booten collected his experiments in a book called “Gyms.” His work reminds us what the ancients understood, and what we keep having to relearn: that the exercise is the point.

希腊人甚至为此创造了一个专有名词：*progymnasmata*（预备修辞训练）。这是一套由浅入深、难度递增的修辞学序列练习，旨在逐步构建学生的论辩能力，正如耐力训练计划锻炼跑者的耐力一般：循序渐进，每一个练习都在为下一阶段的突破筑牢根基。其终极目标不是炮制出一篇现成的文章，而是锻造一颗更强大的心智。康涅狄格大学助理教授、诗人凯尔·布滕（Kyle Booten）在过去几年中致力于借助数字工具复兴这一传统——他开发的计算机辅助工具并非为了让写作变得更容易，而是让写作变得更具挑战，将阻力重新注入到创作行为之中，正如教练不断在杠铃杆上增加杠铃片。他开发了一款强制要求使用生僻词汇的程序；另一项令人叫绝的实验名为“莲花合唱工坊”（Lotus Chorus Workshop），其提示词挑战使用者尝试各种奇特的句式构造，就像一个文学界的 CrossFit 训练营。布滕恰如其分地将这些探索集结成书，题为《健身房》（*Gyms*）。他的研究提醒着我们那些古人早已参透、而我们却在不断遗忘并重新领悟的真理：锻炼过程本身，才是唯一的要义。

Faculty, myself included, have long complained about the outsized influence of athletics on university life. (The Ivy League is, after all, just a football conference.) But, as machines press harder on the academic enterprise, we might do well to look to athletics as a model for the classroom in the age of A.I. I think often about a student I see when I’m swimming laps in the Dartmouth pool. He repeatedly climbs the ladder of the high dive; jumps, rehearsing a few seconds of flight; then climbs it again. There are no athletic scholarships at Dartmouth, and there’s little chance he will dive competitively after graduation. He stays on that board because he’s part of a team, and, to sustain the team, the institution has set aside this space, this time, this lane. That combination—the individual struggle held inside a larger shared commitment—is what will remain with him after graduation. The most valuable part of the athletic experience is what is left when the athlete no longer takes the field.

长期以来，包括我自己在内的大学教职人员，常常抱怨体育竞技在大学生活中占据了过大的比重（毕竟常春藤盟校归根结底只是一个橄榄球体育联盟）。然而，随着机器对学术事业的挤压日益加剧，在人工智能时代，我们或许不妨把体育竞技视作课堂重塑的一个典范模型。在达特茅斯的游泳池畅游时，我常常想起在那里偶遇的一个学生。他一次又一次攀上高台跳水的扶梯，纵身跃下，反复排演那短短几秒钟的滞空飞翔，随后再次爬上跳台。达特茅斯不设立任何体育奖学金，他毕业后继续从事竞技跳水的概率微乎其微。他之所以一次次站上跳板，是因为他是团队的一分子；而为了支撑这支队伍，学校特意划分出这片场地、这段时间、这条泳道。那种将个体的挣扎与搏击融入到更宏大的集体承诺之中的体验，才是他在毕业后终生受用的财富。体育经历中最弥足珍贵的部分，恰恰是当运动员告别赛场后，在灵魂深处所沉淀下来的东西。

Last year, I was among a small group that organized a forum on campus, “Deep Questions,” in which faculty from various disciplines explained to other faculty the problems and mysteries that drove them in their work. One week, I presented the deep question that I’d been struggling with: “What are we doing here?” My colleagues mostly came back to the same answers: we wanted to fuel curiosity, generate a hunger for ideas, create a place where people work together on something difficult and share a transformative experience. I turned again to the spin studio: a group of strangers can, for forty-five minutes, feel like a community, high-fiving one another, shouting in unison, helping one another condition their bodies in a way that they couldn’t on their own. How could we build spin studios for the mind?

去年，我参与组织了一个校内小型论坛“深度之问”（Deep Questions），让来自各个学科的学者向同行们阐释那些驱动他们在研究中不断探索的根本困惑与奥秘。有一周，我抛出了自己苦思冥想的那个深层追问：“我们在这里究竟是在做什么？”同事们的答案大多殊途同归：我们希望激发求知欲，点燃对思想的渴求，创造一个让大家为了攻克棘手难题而通力合作、并共同经历脱胎换骨式蜕变的圣地。我又不由自主地想到了那个动感单车房：一群素昧平生的陌生人，可以在四十五分钟里凝聚成一个紧密的共同体，彼此击掌，齐声呼号，相互扶持着完成单凭个人意志绝难做到的身体重塑。我们又该如何为人类的心智，构筑这样一间动感单车房？

In some ways, the advent of A.I. clarifies our place in the educational equation. Let the machines do what’s mechanical—generating practice problems, flagging errors, grading the responses that are beyond discussion. That only clarifies our real responsibilities: drawing out the quiet student, asking the question nobody thought to ask, forging a community of mind and friendship from the struggles of a classroom. It will be tough. But it will be fun, because you will feel yourself thinking.

在某种程度上，AI 的降临廓清了我们在教育方程式中所处的位置。凡是机械性的工作，不妨通通交给机器代劳——生成练习题、标出错误、为那些毫无争议的标准答案打分。这只会让我们真正的职责变得愈发清晰：引导那些沉默寡言的学生敞开心扉，抛出那个从未有人想过的犀利提问，在共同历经课堂考验与挫折的磨砺中，锻造出心智交融的共同体与深厚情谊。这注定会是一条艰难的道路，但也必将充满乐趣，因为你将真真切切地感受到自己在独立思考。

I think of the teachers who did this for me. When I was an undergraduate, I took a class led by the mathematician Dick Gross. I was a shy and less than confident student, but one day I had a little thought that felt like an idea. I screwed up my courage to ask a question about matrices, those numerical webs that underlie much of abstract algebra. I asked, “Can you have matrices of matrices?” A few classmates turned to look at me—I couldn’t tell whether they thought I had asked the dumbest question in history or merely the strangest. I started to shrink. But Gross’s eyes lit up. He read me and then read the room. What followed was a spontaneous, generous, enthusiastic sketch of matrix theory: its origins, its evolution, and the wonderland of wild and beautiful abstraction to which it belonged. Other classmates chimed in. Gross didn’t just answer my question; he showed the whole room that the question was worth asking, and with it, that all questions were worth asking.

我不由得想起了曾这样点亮过我的师长们。本科求学期间，我曾选修过数学家迪克·格罗斯（Dick Gross）的一门课程。当时的我生性腼腆、缺乏自信，但某天我脑海中冒出了一个自认为有点想法的小念头。我鼓起勇气提了一个关于矩阵——那些构成大部分抽象代数基石的数字网络——的问题。我问道：“矩阵的元素本身，还能是矩阵吗？”有几位同学转过头来看我——我分不清他们是觉得我问出了史上最愚蠢的问题，还是纯粹觉得古怪至极。我顿感无地自容，下意识地想要退缩。然而，格罗斯的眼睛却倏然亮了起来。他看穿了我的局促，也扫视了整个教室。紧接着，他即兴、从容而饱含热情地勾勒出了矩阵理论的壮阔画卷：它的渊源、演进，以及它所隶属的那个狂野而瑰丽的抽象奇境。其他同学也纷纷加入讨论。格罗斯不仅解答了我的疑问，更向全班同学证明了这个提问本身的价值，并随之证明了：世上所有的探索与提问，皆值得被郑重以待。

“We can do that!” is the message. And “do” is the operative word. Some years ago, teaching a course in probability theory, I assigned a heavy problem set near the end of class. The grumbling was louder than usual. Then a student in the front row stood up. He was a defensive tackle on the football team—big, attentive, the kind of student who came to office hours and sometimes stayed so long that it cut into his practice. He turned to face his classmates.

“我们能做到！”这就是其中的真谛，而“做”（do）正是核心的动词。数年前在教授概率论课程时，临近下课，我布置了一套极为繁重的作业题，台下抱怨的叹息声比平时大得多。这时，前排的一位学生站了起来。他是学校橄榄球队的防守截锋——身材魁梧、听课专注，属于那种常来答疑时间请教、甚至常常因讨论太久而耽误训练的学生。他转过身去，面朝全班同学。

“Just suck it up and do the work,” he told them. Aristotle couldn’t have said it better. Neither could Einstein. The physicist, I mean. ♦

“咬紧牙关，把活儿干了就是了。”他对大家说道。即便是亚里士多德，恐怕也不会总结得比这更精辟了。爱因斯坦同样不能——我指的是物理学家爱因斯坦。♦
